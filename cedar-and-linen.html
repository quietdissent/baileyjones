Cedar & Linen

A second-opinion review for renovation finish selections, and the controlled study that tested demand before I built it.

Role
Concept, service design, decision framework, brand, research design and QA.
Build
AI-assisted development under my direction.
Status
Study complete. Concept not pursued.

The problem

A renovation means dozens of finish decisions — tile, paint, counters, fixtures, hardware, lighting — usually made one at a time, often in different stores, on a schedule the contractor sets. Each choice can be fine on its own and wrong in the room. The mistakes show up after installation, when they cost the most to fix.

Designers solve this, but a full design service is more than many renovators want or can pay for. They don’t need someone to choose for them. They need someone to check what they’ve already chosen.

The service

A fixed-scope review. The homeowner sends what they already have:

  • photos or video of the room, and its dimensions
  • inspiration images
  • what’s staying in place
  • the selections they’re considering

They get back a verdict on every decision, each with the reasoning behind it.

Example review, illustrative

KEEP Oak flooring
It anchors the warm palette. Everything else in the room should defer to it.
CHANGE Polished nickel faucet
Switch to brushed brass to match the cabinet hardware already ordered.
RISK Large matte tile, shower floor
Slip rating isn’t listed. Confirm it’s rated for wet floors before ordering.
MISSING Vanity lighting
No fixture chosen. Plan sconces at face height, not a single overhead light.

Key decisions

A fixed vocabulary of four verdicts

WhyOpen-ended design feedback is hard to act on and easy to argue with. Four verdicts make a review scannable in seconds, and every verdict has to carry a reason and, where it applies, a specific correction.

TradeoffSome nuance gets forced into a category. The written reasoning carries it.

Uncertainty gets its own verdict

WhySome problems can’t be confirmed from photos: slip ratings, the color temperature of a bulb, how a sample reads at full scale. Labeling those as risks, with what to check, is more honest than guessing in either direction.

Review what wasn’t chosen

WhyThe costliest gaps are often decisions nobody made — no lighting plan, no transition between floors. A review that only grades what was submitted would miss them.

Testing it first

Delivering this well would take serious design knowledge, and I wasn’t going to spend months building it on a guess. So before building the service, I built a study to find out whether homeowners wanted it and what they’d pay: interviews with homeowners, plus a controlled test of the offer itself.

Outcome

The study did its job: it answered the question before the expensive part began. The answer didn’t justify building the service, so I stopped there.

That’s the result I’m most satisfied with. Quiet Dissent taught me what it costs to build everything before testing the riskiest assumption. Here, the riskiest assumption went first.
