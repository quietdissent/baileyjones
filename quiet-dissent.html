Quiet Dissent

One connected system for a one-person advisory practice, from a stranger’s first visit to a client’s ongoing engagement.

Role
Sole designer. Research, positioning, information architecture, interaction and visual design, content and QA.
Build
AI-assisted development under my direction: I set the architecture, requirements and design, and reviewed what was built against them.
Stack
Next.js, React, Supabase, Clerk, Tailwind, Vercel
Status
Complete system, not launched to clients.

The problem

Owners of small service businesses — home services, healthcare, legal and professional services, roughly 5 to 50 people — lose work in places they can’t see. Calls ring out after hours. Web leads wait a day for a reply. Staff spend hours on tasks software could take. Most owners have already tried a tool or two. Nothing is connected, and nobody is steering.

A practice selling into that has its own problem: what it sells is a diagnosis, and a diagnosis is invisible until someone pays for it. So the design question was how to make the problem concrete for an owner before the first conversation, and then keep everything after that conversation in one place.

Constraints

  • One person doing the selling, the work and the support. Anything that needed a team to run was out.
  • An audience with little time and no patience for jargon.
  • Every number shown to an owner had to survive the owner checking it.

The system

Each tool exists to move an owner one step: from noticing a problem, to sizing it, to talking about it, to working on it. They share one visual language so they read as one company rather than five products.

  1. Attract
    • Marketing siteFrames the problem in an owner’s words before naming a service.
    • Outreach toolkitInternal. Reads a business’s public reviews for signs of service gaps and tracks prospects.
  2. Diagnose
    • Readiness quizSeven questions in three parts, ending in where the business is most likely losing time or leads.
    • ROI calculatorSizes missed-call revenue from the owner’s own numbers.
  3. Engage
    • Consultation requestAsks for the biggest operational headache in the owner’s words, not a menu of services.
  4. Deliver
    • Client portalDocuments, messages and scheduling, organised by engagement.
    • Engagement documentsThree tiers, five or six defined documents each.

Dashed line marks an internal tool.

Key decisions

Let owners diagnose themselves first

The quiz asks seven questions in three parts: revenue leaks, operational bottlenecks, and appetite for change. Every question includes an answer for “this doesn’t apply to my operation.”

WhyA forced choice produces a false answer, and a false answer produces a false result. The escape option keeps results honest and shows which questions don’t fit a given trade.

TradeoffFewer complete scores, and some results are less specific.

Make the calculator show its work

Choosing an industry fills in typical call volume, miss rate, job value and close rate. Every input stays editable, the recovery assumption is deliberately conservative, and the sources behind each default open inside the tool.

WhyOwners distrust ROI calculators because most are built to produce a big number. Showing the assumptions, and letting owners replace them with their own, turns the result from a sales claim into their own estimate.

TradeoffSmaller headline numbers.

One portal, two roles

Clients and the admin share one data model. Documents, messages and scheduled calls are scoped to each client; the admin side invites clients, publishes documents and runs every conversation. A new client’s account links itself to their record when they finish signing up, and messages update in real time.

WhyA solo operator can’t afford context scattered across email, shared folders and calendar threads, and a client shouldn’t have to hunt for what they were sent.

TradeoffMuch more to build than a shared folder. It only pays off on engagements that run for months.

Design the deliverables, not just the screens

Work is structured as three engagements: an assessment, an implementation, and an ongoing advisory retainer. Each has a defined set of documents, from the service agreement to the performance report, and each document is labeled by audience: client, internal or shared.

WhyScope is what small clients fear most. A visible list of what they get, and when, makes the engagement concrete before they sign.

One visual language across every tool

A dark, quiet opening gives way to warm, light pages. A serif carries the voice, a sans carries the interface, and one muted green does all the emphasis.

WhyThe tools were built at different times as separate deployments. Shared type, color and spacing are what make them feel like one practice.

What I learned

I built the whole system before testing its riskiest assumption: whether owners would take the first conversation. Everything after that conversation was ready. The conversation itself was never tested.

The next project inverted the order. Test the demand first, build second.
